You have read the books, you have tried breathing, you tried "accepting what I feel", and it worked. Other times, the same technique, in front of the same person, and it did nothing at all.

That is the moment many people give up: if it doesn't work, then either the technique is an invention, or something is wrong with them. I made that question the subject of my doctoral thesis and, years earlier, of six published studies. The answer I found there, and then met again in the office, is less dramatic and much more usable:

> "I tried everything I know. Why doesn't anything work?"

## The problem isn't the technique, it's the choice

When an emotion rises, the mind looks for anything that discharges the tension and grabs the first strategy within reach. Usually we don't apply a chosen technique, we apply a mixture: a little reappraisal, a little suppression, a little self-blame, all in the same minute.

We tested that directly, in a study published in 2018 with colleagues from Boston University and Northeastern: people left to regulate their emotions on their own, without instructions, almost never used one clean strategy. [They combined several strategies at once](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41811-018-0026-9), and the combination mattered more than any single piece of it.

My doctoral thesis, defended in 2020 at Babeș-Bolyai University under supervisor Prof. Mircea Miclea, was about exactly this mechanism: "Selecția strategiilor de reglare emoțională și intensitatea afectivă" (Selection of emotion regulation strategies and affective intensity). The more intense the emotion, the poorer the choice of strategy becomes. Which means it breaks exactly where you need it most.

## What the six studies show, in plain language

All six share one subject: how we regulate emotions under pressure. I signed them as first author, some together with Aurora Szentagotai from Babeș-Bolyai, others with researchers at Boston University and Massachusetts General Hospital, and together they have been cited more than 770 times in the specialist literature.

- **Swallowing anger costs something** — in an experiment with a frustrating task, people who reappraised the situation felt less anger and persisted longer at the task than those who suppressed their anger or simply accepted it ([The effect of emotion regulation strategies on anger](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2010.11.011), Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2011).
- **What you do in minute two spreads** — regulating anger and sadness measurably changed the decisions taken immediately afterwards ([Effect of regulating anger and sadness on decision-making](https://doi.org/10.1080/16506073.2016.1203354), Cognitive Behaviour Therapy, 2016).
- **Cravings respond to strategy** — reappraisal worked better than suppression and better than acceptance at reducing cigarette craving and the attention pulled by cigarette cues ([Effects of emotion regulation strategies on smoking craving, attentional bias, and task persistence](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2012.02.010), Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2012). It isn't about cigarettes, it is about any impulse you feed by looking at it.
- **The thought you push comes back grinding** — suppressing unpleasant thoughts was linked to rumination and, through rumination, to depressive symptoms (Thought suppression, depressive rumination and depression, 2009). What matters isn't only that the thought is there, it's what you do with it.
- **Rigid rules have a measurable price** — beliefs like "I must not fail" and "everyone must like me" predicted emotional distress better than the events themselves (The role of irrational beliefs, brooding and reflective pondering in predicting distress, 2011). Analyzing something forever is not the same as thinking that clarifies.

## What a laboratory does not show

Here I want to be precise, because this is where research is most often sold as more than it can deliver.

The results above show what can happen, not what will happen for you. In a laboratory the emotion is induced and it lasts a few minutes; in your life anger arrives with history, with someone sleeping in the next room, and with consequences that are still there the next day. What I can promise is not an outcome. It is that the mechanism we work with in the office is not a pretty hypothesis, it is a tested one.

## What changes when you choose according to intensity

Two things you can check against your own experience:

- **At low intensity, processing helps** — you stay with what you feel, you name it, you see what triggered it. At high intensity the same move sinks you: first you need to bring the wave down, and only then look at it.
- **The mixture exhausts** — moving from breathing to self-encouragement to reproach inside the same minute isn't flexibility, it's panic with several tools. One strategy applied cleanly, for enough time, gives you more information than five applied halfway.

This is not a fixed recipe. It means that a person who can read your intensity, in a session, live, may choose something different from the book you read two years ago.

## Why you can't train this alone, forever

The choice of strategy has to be made in exactly the moment when your judgment is most affected. It is like asking someone, in the middle of the crisis, to be the technician of their own crisis.

In the office it looks different: we take one real episode apart, we see which strategy you used and what it cost you, we practice another one at a moment when the emotion is not at its maximum, and then you take it into the real situation. That is [CBT and REBT](/en/psychotherapy/): the event, the interpretation, the emotion, the demand underneath them. And choosing a strategy is trained through repetition under varied conditions, not through understanding, which is what [head vs. heart](/en/blog/head-vs-heart/) is about.

If you want to see what the work looks like on patterns rather than on isolated techniques, the article on [intense emotions and patterns that repeat in relationships](/en/blog/intense-emotions-and-difficult-relationships/) describes exactly that.
