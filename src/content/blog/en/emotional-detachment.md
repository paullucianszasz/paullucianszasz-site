You have heard both pieces of advice, sometimes in the same week. The first: get fully involved, love without reserves, want things with everything you have. The second, after a period in which you did get involved and paid for it: detach, stop caring, become waterproof.

They are presented as opposites. In the office they look strikingly similar: both are attempts to stop feeling a pain, and both cost you something. The difference is not how much you care. It is what gets added on top of the fact that you care.

> "I do not want to care about anything anymore."

## Two routes, one starting point

Clinging says: if I get what I want, I am okay. Detachment says: if I want nothing, I cannot be hurt. Both tie your peace to an arrangement with reality — one tries to persuade it, the other quits before it starts.

What they share is a rigid demand about how things have to be. On one side: "they must love me". On the other: "I must not need anyone". The second one sounds like freedom, but it is the same kind of demand, turned negative. And an unmet demand produces exactly what it was meant to prevent.

## The first trap: a desire that turns into a demand

Albert Ellis put first on his list of the beliefs that disturb us precisely this one: the conviction that I must be loved or approved by the people whose opinion matters to me. The desire is not the problem — that part is human and healthy. The problem starts when a desire becomes an obligation with "otherwise it is awful and I cannot bear it" attached to it.

It happens silently, in a few seconds:

- **In a relationship** — "I want them to answer" becomes "they must answer, otherwise it means they do not love me".
- **At work** — "I want the project" becomes "I must get it, otherwise I am a failure".
- **With myself** — "I want peace at home" becomes "it has to be like that, otherwise I cannot go on".

In **REBT**, three things follow from the rigid demand: catastrophising (what happened is the worst possible thing), low frustration tolerance (I cannot bear this) and the global rating (if I lose, I am nothing). The chain is described step by step in [how therapy works: the ABC model](/en/blog/understanding-how-therapy-works-the-abc-model/). Only one thing matters here: the suffering does not come from the desire, but from the "must" glued to it.

## The second trap: "I do not care anymore"

When clinging hurts too much, many people choose the opposite pole. Attachment research has a name for that strategy: **deactivation** — inhibiting the need for closeness, minimizing your own needs, what Bowlby called "compulsive self-reliance". The research shows something that matters here: the opposite strategies — intensifying and suppressing — both lead to dysfunctional emotional experience. One is not wise and the other foolish; both carry the same cost.

The Buddhist tradition warned about the same thing in different vocabulary. Equanimity (upekkhā) has a "near enemy": **indifference**, a state that resembles it from outside and contradicts it from inside. The monk and translator Bhikkhu Bodhi stresses that equanimity is not indifference towards other people, but steadiness in the face of what you cannot control — and that it does not cancel the other warm attitudes, it completes them.

I say this as a parallel, not as a synthesis. REBT and Buddhism are different traditions with different aims; the fact that both flagged this trap does not make them one system.

## What the middle actually is

In **REBT**, the healthy position is called a **flexible preference**, and its structure has two halves, both said out loud: "I want this intensely and I will do everything that is mine to do. It does not have to happen. If it does not happen, it will hurt a great deal — and I can carry that pain."

The first half is the desire, kept whole. The second is the denial of the demand. Without the first you become resigned; without the second you stay clinging. A "I want" said on its own is not enough, because the demand can be hiding inside it. That is exactly what we check in a session: when you say "I do not care", what exactly stopped mattering to you, and what still hurts.

In practice the difference shows in what you do after a loss. The clinging person negotiates, insists, compares. The closed-off person declares they never wanted it anyway. The person who has learned to want without demanding feels the blow and still acts according to what matters to them.

## It does not mean less emotion

The word "rational" is widely misunderstood: many people believe REBT recommends a cold-headed life without feelings. It does not. REBT distinguishes an unpleasant emotion that helps you from one that blocks you: concern and anxiety, sadness and depression, disappointment and resentment. The goal is not a weaker emotion, but one that does not make your decisions for you.

Buddhism reaches a similar distinction from another direction: pleasure can be felt fully without clinging, and pain fully without condemnation. If your only options were to feel less or to feel nothing, you have chosen both versions of the same flight.

If the idea of "reason" always feels opposed to the idea of "living", the article on [reason and experience](/en/blog/head-vs-heart/) was written about exactly that confusion.

## How this is actually worked on

- **One episode, not a whole life** — we take a recent reaction and take it apart: what happened, what you took it to mean, which demand came next, what you did to protect yourself.
- **The two-part formula** — we build the alternative sentence together, in your words, and practice it in real moments, not only in the office.
- **A simple exercise between sessions** — when you catch the thought "I do not care anymore", write down what that "not" actually wanted. That is where the work is.
- **No promises** — I do not work with "you will stop feeling". I work with how often your decisions come from a place of fear.
